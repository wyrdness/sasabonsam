## Overview

The Sasabonsam (also Asasabonsam, Sasabonsum, Shamansum) is a vampiric forest entity from the folklore of the Akan peoples of southern Ghana, Côte d'Ivoire and Togo, including the Ashanti, Fante, and related groups. It is canonically described — most influentially in R. S. Rattray's 1920s Ashanti ethnographies — as a hairy, monstrous humanoid that lives high in the trees of the forest, with iron teeth, blood-shot eyes, very long legs, and feet that point both forwards and backwards (or that end in hooks). Sasabonsam dangles its long legs from a branch and snares any traveller who walks beneath. Within Akan demonology it is closely linked to obayifo, the soul-eating witch: in some accounts Sasabonsam is the master of obayifo, in others the two terms are used as near-synonyms. Sasabonsam functions in Akan tradition as a supernatural enforcer of the forest and of taboo: the figure punishes hunters who break sacred-day prohibitions or trespass on forbidden ground. The Akan tradition was carried into the Caribbean by enslaved Akan people in the 17th and 18th centuries, where it contributed to local Jamaican and Surinamese vampire-witch beliefs. A widely-reproduced wooden carving of a Sasabonsam, collected by L. W. G. Malcolm in 1928 from a shrine of the Aowin people of southwestern Ghana, is held by the Pitt Rivers Museum in Oxford.

## Etymology

- **Origin**: Akan (Twi)
- **Meaning**: From sasa ('spirit, ghost, vital force', especially of an aggrieved dead) + bonsam ('devil, evil being') — broadly 'spirit-devil' or 'wraith-devil'
- **First use**: Recorded ethnographically by R. S. Rattray in his Ashanti studies (1923, 1927)

## Theories & Explanations

### Paranormal / Cryptozoological

**Akan forest-devil tradition** _(Akan religious tradition, Rattray as ethnographic interlocutor)_

Within Akan religion Sasabonsam is a real and active forest entity, often paired with obayifo witches as part of a wider category of harmful spiritual beings.

### Skeptical

**Folkloric and social function** _(Folklorists and anthropologists of West Africa)_

Sasabonsam is best understood as a folkloric figure that enforces forest taboos, dramatises fears of the wilderness, and articulates Akan ideas about witchcraft and aggrieved dead.
